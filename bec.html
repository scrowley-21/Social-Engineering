Business Email Compromise (BEC)

BEC is a type of fraud where attackers impersonate or take over a trusted business email account to trick an employee, vendor, or customer into sending money or sensitive information.[1]

By Windy Xiong

Why it slips past security

BEC emails usually have no malicious links or attachments. They're just text asking for something, so traditional security filters often let them through.[4]

What drives it

BEC exploits trust that already exists in a known sender or role, like an executive, a vendor, or a finance contact. The attacker makes the request seem routine, legitimate, and time-sensitive so the target acts before verifying. It's basically pretexting over email, and pretexting showed up in 30% of social engineering incidents in Verizon's 2025 report.[4]

Item 1

How does BEC work?

BEC attackers use pressure to get around a company's normal approval process. Three tactics show up in almost every attempt:

Authority

Posing as the CEO or CFO. Employees don't want to say no to the boss, or slow them down.

Urgency

"This must be paid today." A tight deadline leaves no room for the usual checks.

Confidentiality

"Keep this between us." Secrecy stops the victim from asking a coworker, who might notice something's off.

Types of BEC

CEO / executive fraud

Impersonating a senior leader to request urgent wire transfers or gift card purchases.

Vendor email compromise

Also called invoice fraud: spoofing or hijacking a supplier's account to redirect payments.

Account compromise

Taking over an employee's real mailbox and sending requests to their contacts and vendors.

Data theft

Targeting HR or finance for employee records, W-2 tax forms, or financial data.[1]

Example

What a BEC email can look like

This example is made up. The company, people, and addresses are fictional.

Example CEO fraud email
From: Daniel Reyes <d.reyes@harborline-co.com1>
To: Jordan Lee <j.lee@harborlineco.com>
Subject: Quick favor - need this today

Jordan,

I'm stuck in back-to-back meetings at the conference and can't take calls2. We're finalizing a pending acquisition and I need you to process a wire of $48,750 to the account below before 3pm today3.

This deal is confidential until it's announced, so please don't mention it to anyone4, including finance leadership.

Use the new banking details attached5 and reply once it's sent.

Thanks,
Daniel
Sent from my iPhone

Red flags in this email

  • 1Lookalike domain. "harborline-co.com" vs. the real "harborlineco.com." One extra hyphen.
  • 2Blocks phone verification. The attacker makes sure you can't just call to check.
  • 3Urgent deadline. Pressure to skip the normal approval steps.
  • 4Asks for secrecy. Cuts you off from coworkers who might catch the scam.
  • 5New payment details. Any sudden change to where money goes should be verified.
Item 2

Examples of BEC

A

The overdue invoice

An attacker compromises or spoofs a known vendor's email and sends a message about an "overdue invoice." It includes real details to seem authentic and demands immediate payment to updated bank account details.

B

The out-of-office CEO

A scammer impersonates the CEO while they're out of the office and emails a finance employee asking for an urgent, confidential wire transfer for a "pending acquisition," while discouraging any phone verification.

C

The hijacked mailbox

An attacker takes over an employee's real email and slips a fraud request into an ongoing conversation, like changing payroll direct deposit or payment details, at a moment when it seems completely normal.

Item 3

How to prevent BEC

Strict verification

Require secondary confirmation for wire transfers, payment changes, and sensitive data requests, by calling a trusted phone number already on file.[1]

Zero-trust culture and dual approval

Encourage employees to question unexpected or out-of-band requests, and require two people to approve high-value payments.

Regular training

Run security awareness training that teaches people to notice urgency, secrecy, and emotional pressure.

Warning signs at a glance

Lookalike addresses

One letter or hyphen off from a real company or coworker.

Sudden payment changes

"We've switched banks, please use these new details."

Urgency

Deadlines that leave no time to check with anyone.

Secrecy

Being told not to tell anyone or not to call.

Conclusion

Business email compromise stands out because it relies on human connection instead of malware or other technical hacks. It's one of the most financially damaging forms of cybercrime. The good news is that the fixes are mostly process, not technology: verifying payment requests through a separate channel, requiring dual approval, and training employees to slow down when an email feels urgent or secretive.
