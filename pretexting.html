Pretexting

Pretexting is a targeted form of social engineering where an attacker invents a scenario (the "pretext") to manipulate a victim into giving up important information or granting access they shouldn't.[1]

By Sienna Crowley-Wallin

The core idea: a pretexting attack runs on fake trust. The attacker's whole job is convincing you they're who they say they are, and that they have a real and usually urgent reason to need what they're asking for.

The term came from the private investigation industry. In the U.S., using pretexting to get someone's financial records is illegal under the Gramm-Leach-Bliley Act.[1]

How an attack unfolds

The lifecycle of a pretexting attack

One phone call or email is rarely the end goal. The information from a pretext is usually a stepping stone to something bigger: attackers often use details from one person to sound more believable to the next.[2]

01

Reconnaissance

Research the target using LinkedIn, the company site, social media, and org charts.

02

Build the pretext

Pick a role (IT tech, vendor, auditor, new hire) and a story that fits what they learned.

03

Make contact

Call, email, text, or show up in person, using real names and details to sound legit.

04

Build trust and apply pressure

Use authority, urgency, or sympathy so the victim doesn't stop to check.

05

The ask

Request a password, a file, a payment, or a door held open.

↓ what that first win leads to ↓

Credential harvesting

Stolen logins let the attacker into email, cloud apps, or internal systems.

Secondary baiting

Inside knowledge makes the next phishing email or call far more convincing.

Physical piggybacking

Walking in behind an employee gets the attacker past badge readers and into secure areas.

Broader compromise: data theft, fraud, or a full network breach

Item 1

How does pretexting work?

Pretexting is mostly psychology. The attacker isn't hacking a system. They're hacking someone's judgment by setting up a situation where saying yes feels like the normal, helpful, or safe thing to do. Three levers come up over and over:

Authority

Posing as a manager, auditor, police officer, or IT admin. People are trained to cooperate with authority and don't want to look difficult by questioning it.

Urgency

"The system goes down in an hour." "This invoice is overdue." A deadline cuts out the time people would normally spend double-checking.

Empathy

"I'm new and my boss will kill me if I don't get this done." Asking for help makes the victim want to be the one who fixes the problem.

Familiarity

Using real names, project details, and internal lingo from their research. If someone knows that much, it feels like they must belong.

Common pretexting techniques

Impersonation

Pretending to be a specific coworker, executive, or vendor the victim already knows.

Fake IT or help desk

Calling about a "security issue" and asking for a password or remote access to fix it.[2]

Vishing (voice pretexting)

Running the pretext over the phone, where tone of voice builds trust and there's no email to inspect.

Physical pretexting

Showing up as a delivery driver, contractor, or inspector to get into a building.

Survey or research ruse

Posing as a researcher or recruiter and asking "harmless" questions that reveal internal details.

Item 2

Examples of pretexting

A

The "overdue invoice"

An attacker compromises or spoofs a known vendor's email and sends a message about an overdue invoice. It includes detailed project notes to seem real, and asks for immediate payment to "updated" bank account details. (This is also a business email compromise attack.)

B

Fake IT support

Someone calls claiming to be from IT or a software vendor and says there's an urgent system issue. To fix it, they need your login credentials, right now.

C

The delivery driver or auditor

An attacker in a uniform or carrying a clipboard asks to be let in, or just follows an employee through a secure door. Nobody wants to be rude, so nobody asks.

Item 3

How to prevent pretexting

Strict verification

Require a second confirmation for data sharing and wire transfers, like calling back a trusted phone number already on file, never the number in the message.

A "zero-trust" culture

Make it normal for employees to question unexpected or out-of-band requests, no matter how senior the person asking seems to be.

Regular training

Run security awareness training focused on spotting emotional manipulation and verifying identities before acting.[2]

Conclusion

Pretexting relies on human psychology instead of technical exploits, and that's exactly why it's one of the most effective ways for attackers to break into organizations. No software update fixes it. The strongest defenses are steady vigilance and verification steps that everyone actually follows, even when the request seems urgent or comes from someone important.
