Phishing

Phishing is one of the most common types of social engineering. It happens when someone tries to trick people into giving away personal information by pretending to be a trusted person or organization, through email, texts, or other messages.[1]

By Kiara Villagran-Santos

The goal of this page is to explain what phishing is, how these scams work, and what you can do to avoid becoming a victim.

Item 1

Understanding phishing and how scams work

Phishing gets its name from "fishing": the scammer throws out bait and waits for someone to bite. It's a form of social engineering because it targets people's trust instead of breaking into a computer directly.[3]

Scammers pretend to be companies you already deal with, like your bank, a delivery service, a streaming account, or even your school or employer. They copy logos, colors, and the tone of real messages so the fake looks right at a glance.

Email phishing

The classic version: a fake email with a link to a lookalike login page or a harmful attachment.

Smishing (texts)

Texts about a missed package, a toll you owe, or a locked account, with a link to "fix it."

Social media

Fake giveaways, hacked friend accounts, or DMs pretending to be customer support.

Vishing (calls)

Phone calls from someone claiming to be your bank's fraud team or a government agency.

The emotional hooks

Phishing messages are built to make you react before you think. They lean on fear ("your account will be closed"), urgency ("respond within 24 hours"), or curiosity ("see who viewed your profile").

What happens if you bite

Clicking a bad link or sharing info can lead to stolen accounts, identity theft, or malware on your device. One stolen password often unlocks other accounts if you reuse it.

Example

What a phishing email can look like

This example is made up. The company and addresses are fictional.

Example phishing email
From: Northpoint Bank Security <alerts@northpoint-secure-login.co1>
To: you@email.com
Subject: URGENT: Your account has been suspended2

Dear Valued Customer,3

We detected unusual sign-in activity on your account. For your protection, access has been temporarily suspended.

You must verify your identity within 24 hours4 or your account will be permanently closed.

Verify My Account5

Please have your card number, PIN, and online banking password6 ready.

Thank you,
Northpoint Security Team

Red flags in this email

  • 1Weird sender address. The domain isn't the bank's real one, just something that sounds close.
  • 2Scary subject line. All caps and "suspended" are meant to make you panic.
  • 3Generic greeting. Your real bank knows your name. CISA lists "Dear Valued Customer" as a strong warning sign.[3]
  • 4Fake deadline. Pressure to act fast so you don't check.
  • 5A button to "verify." It leads to a fake login page that steals what you type.
  • 6Asking for sensitive info. Real banks won't ask for your PIN or password by email.
Item 2

Recognizing phishing and protecting yourself

Warning signs

Unfamiliar sender

An email address that doesn't match the company, or a name you don't recognize.

Unexpected links or attachments

Especially ones you weren't expecting. Hover over a link to see where it really goes.

Requests for passwords or money info

Legit companies don't ask for these over email or text.

"There's a problem with your account"

Messages saying something needs immediate attention should be checked carefully, not trusted.

Verify before you act. If a message says there's a problem, don't use the link or number in it. Go to the company's official website or app yourself, or call the number on the back of your card.

Item 3

Phishing awareness, training, and prevention

Learning how phishing works is one of the best defenses, because you can recognize a scam before you fall for it. That's why many businesses run simulated phishing emails: fake phishing messages sent to employees on purpose, to teach them what to look for in a safe setting.

But not all phishing emails are equally easy to spot. The NIST Phish Scale, a method from the National Institute of Standards and Technology, helps trainers rate how hard a phishing email is for people to detect. It looks at two things:[4]

Cues

The clues in the email that give it away. NIST groups them into five types: errors, technical indicators, visual presentation, language and content, and common tactics.[4] More cues = easier to spot.

+

Premise alignment

How well the email's story fits the reader's real life and job. An "invoice" email sent to someone in accounting fits well. Better fit = harder to spot.

=

Detection difficulty

Together, these rate the email as easier or harder to catch. This helps explain why some people click on a simulated phish and others don't.

So a sloppy email full of typos about a prize you never entered is easy to catch. A clean email about something you're actually expecting is much harder. That's why personal awareness, employee training, and security tools like spam filters all matter. None of them catches everything alone.

Conclusion

Phishing is a serious online threat because scammers take advantage of people's trust to steal information. But you can lower your chances of falling for these scams by learning the warning signs and being careful with unexpected messages. Hopefully this page helps you understand how phishing works and gives you tips you can actually use to stay safer online.
